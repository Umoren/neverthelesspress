# Nevertheless Press Magazine Art Direction

This file is the visual counterpart to the writing rules.

Use it before turning any issue into a page. The goal is to preserve the magazine art while making the publication repeatable.

## What Nevertheless Press Is

Nevertheless Press is a gospel publication that turns sermon-derived material, Scripture, and personal reflection into digital magazine issues.

It is not a church blog.

It is not a sermon-notes page.

It is not a devotional dump.

Each issue should feel like an editorial object. The reader should feel that the copy, typography, layout, Scripture, pull quotes, colors, and motion belong to one issue.

## The Non-Negotiable

Do not lose the magazine art.

A reusable system is useful only if it does not flatten the publication into a generic article template.

Every issue needs its own visual argument. The design should help the reader feel what the issue is saying.

## What The Design Must Do

The design should deepen the message, not decorate it.

For every issue, the LLM should identify:

1. The human problem.
2. The doctrinal center.
3. The Bible scenes or references that carry the argument.
4. The tension that needs visual treatment.
5. The strongest pull quotes.
6. The closing movement.

Then the issue should be divided into spreads.

One spread should do one job.

## Spread Logic

A spread is not just a section of text. It is a visual unit with a purpose.

Common spread jobs:

- Open the ache.
- Name the tension.
- Show the Bible scene.
- Establish the theological claim.
- Hold the paradox.
- Give the cross its weight.
- Move into practice.
- Close with a reframing line.

Each spread should have a clear hierarchy:

- issue marker or section number
- label
- headline
- body copy
- visual artifact, pull quote, Scripture stamp, or motif

If a spread has no visual job, it should probably be merged, cut, or redesigned.

## House Style

The house style should feel editorial, gospel-centered, and issue-driven.

Use:

- strong editorial typography
- generous spacing
- clear section numbers
- Scripture references as visual anchors
- pull quotes as graphic moments
- issue-specific palettes
- long-scroll magazine pacing
- intentional motion only where it helps the reading experience

Avoid:

- generic blog cards
- stock devotional softness
- random gradients
- floating blobs
- decorative elements that do not come from the issue
- over-designed sections that fight the copy
- making every issue use the same visual mood

## Typography Rules

Typography should carry the magazine feeling.

Use an editorial serif for big issue moments when it fits the issue. Use a restrained sans or monospace for metadata, labels, captions, references, and technical structure.

Headlines should feel composed, not random. Body copy should remain readable and calm.

Do not use default-looking typography when the issue needs art direction.

Do not make body copy small just to make a layout look more designed.

Do not let typography become harder to read than the prose.

## Color Rules

The base palette can stay close to the existing house:

- ink
- cream
- gold

Each issue should add its own accent palette.

The palette must come from the issue's mood.

Color should create atmosphere and structure. It should not become decoration for its own sake.

Avoid one-note palettes. Avoid default purple/blue AI-looking gradients. Avoid using dark mode as a shortcut for seriousness.

## Scripture Treatment

Scripture should feel handled with weight.

Good scripture treatments:

- verse as a full-width editorial moment
- Scripture reference as a small stamp
- verse fragments used as section anchors
- callout blocks that let the text breathe
- marginal references that feel like study notes

Bad scripture treatments:

- dumping verses into plain paragraphs with no visual hierarchy
- using Scripture as decoration
- turning every verse into the same pull quote style
- burying the verse that carries the argument

If the issue depends on a verse, the design should make that verse visible.

## Motion Rules

Motion should be restrained.

Use it for:

- gentle entry of spreads
- slow reveal of section numbers
- subtle scripture underline or highlight
- small transitions that support reading pace

Avoid:

- motion that calls attention to itself
- constant animation
- generic fade-in everywhere
- interactions that make the issue feel like a product demo instead of a gospel magazine

## Image And Motif Rules

Use visual motifs that come from the issue.

Examples:

- garden
- table
- doorway
- handwritten margin notes
- underlined Scripture
- distance closing
- light entering a room
- folded paper
- marked Bible pages

Avoid:

- cheesy Christian stock imagery
- generic smiling friends
- hands reaching in soft focus
- random crosses as decoration
- AI-looking heavenly glow
- images that say the obvious instead of deepening the issue

## Working Process

Before implementation, create an issue art brief.

The brief should include:

- issue title
- core thesis
- emotional problem
- doctrinal center
- Bible scenes
- visual mood
- palette
- motifs
- spread list
- pull quote candidates
- Scripture treatment
- motion notes
- what to avoid

After the brief, build the issue component.

After the component, run the build and review the page against three questions:

1. Does it still feel like a magazine?
2. Does the design deepen the issue's message?
3. Did the design preserve readability?

## Art Brief: The Friend Jesus

Issue title:

`The Friend Jesus`

Core thesis:

Jesus does not only save people from sin. He brings them into friendship with God.

Emotional problem:

Loneliness. A person can have attention, conversations, and proximity to people while still feeling unknown.

Personal note:

The issue includes Samuel's own experience of loneliness, a fresh breakup, job loss, and other unwanted circumstances. The design should honor this without making the page melodramatic.

Doctrinal center:

John 15:12-15. Jesus calls his people friends while remaining Lord.

Bible scenes and references:

- Garden of Eden
- Adam and Eve hiding
- God calling first
- Moses spoken to face to face
- Abraham called God's friend
- the Word becoming flesh
- Jesus naming his people friends
- the cross as the proof of friendship

Visual mood:

Warm, intimate, quiet, honest, spacious.

Palette:

- cream
- ink
- gold
- deep green

Possible motifs:

- a table with one open seat
- a doorway or threshold
- garden texture
- margin notes
- underlined Scripture
- distance closing across the page
- quiet room light
- handwritten friendship language

Avoid:

- cheesy friendship imagery
- generic smiling people
- soft pastel devotional design
- random hands reaching
- sentimental typography
- making Jesus feel casual

Suggested spread list:

1. Hero: attention is not friendship.
2. Personal note: loneliness, pain, and discovery.
3. Garden of Eden: God began with nearness.
4. Across the Bible: God kept moving toward people.
5. John 15: Jesus says it plainly.
6. Friend, but still Lord: intimacy with reverence.
7. Cross: the proof of friendship.
8. Practice: how believers should love people.
9. Faith: how a person becomes a friend of God.
10. Close: he brings his people near.

Pull quote candidates:

- `You can have attention and still not have friendship.`
- `Jesus calls his people friends.`
- `Friendship with Jesus is intimate and reverent.`
- `His friendship led him to the cross.`
- `The gospel answer to loneliness is this: the Son of God came near.`

Scripture treatment:

John 15:12-15 should be the central Scripture spread. Genesis 3:8-9 should receive a scene-based treatment. John 15:13 should be visually tied to the cross section.

Motion notes:

Use slow, quiet reveals. Let section numbers and verse references appear with restraint. Avoid anything playful or overly smooth.
